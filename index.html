How this works — computed, never generated
Every date on the right is computed in code from Tex. Prop. Code §51.002 — never estimated, never generated by AI. A notice of sale states its own sale date; this tool never invents one. Projected dates are labeled projected. In production you'd upload the notice itself; extraction reads the type, dates and parties — confidence-scored, and you confirm every field.

    What gets sent, and what the guard checks

    The one exception to "nothing leaves this page," and it only runs if you click: your sentence above (nothing else) is sent to Anthropic's Claude Haiku model through this site's server — Anthropic's standard API policy deletes inputs and outputs within about 30 days, with stated policy and legal exceptions, and this demo itself stores nothing. The model may fix grammar, spelling and structure only. A deterministic server-side check rejects output containing guarded tokens you didn't type — digits, written numbers (zero through ninety, dozen, hundred through trillion), month names, percent and money words. It is a narrow token guard, not a full fact-checker, which is why you review and approve every word before it's used. The drafts above work with no AI at all.

    What the software does, what AI does and does not, sources, editorial ownership and privacy — the disclosure box a production version would publish.
    
          
      
    
    
    
      
      
        
    • What the software does: computes statutory deadlines from recorded notice dates, in code. Dates are never produced by a language model.
      • 
        
    • What AI does (production design): translates legal boilerplate into plain language, from a fixed glossary, with the statute cited beside every sentence. In this demo, its two live jobs are the sample-document extraction and the sentence polish — both optional, both gated by code.
      • 
        
    • What AI does not do: give legal advice, predict outcomes, or select remedies. Straight talk on data: extraction means your uploaded notice IS transmitted to the model provider — production redacts names and addresses before transmission, discloses the provider, and sets a retention policy, the same way TX Tax discloses its OpenAI use.
      • 
        
    • Sources: Harris County Clerk foreclosure postings, Texas Property Code §51.002, county auction records. In production, every row links to the underlying filing.
      • 
        
    • Human owner (production requirement): a named editor owns the guidance copy and a corrections log; templates ship only after documented review by Texas housing counsel. Neither exists for this concept demo — and saying so is the point of a trust receipt.
      • 
        
    • Privacy (production design): individual owners are never surfaced by name in the county picture — addresses appear only to the person who searched their own. In this demo, the rule shows up as absence: the extraction schema has no fields for the homeowner's name or address, and the sample documents mark them [REMOVED].
